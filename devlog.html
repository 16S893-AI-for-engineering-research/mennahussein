Oct 5, 2026 Figure update shipped Model comparison

Figure 3: Sonnet vs. the current model

The first attempt produced a polished page, but its trajectory did not match the paper. The second pass shifted the emphasis from presenting a plausible result to checking the paper, reconstructing its numerical experiment, and measuring the difference. The improvement was not just a better-looking curve: it was a more reproducible and more carefully qualified result.

Scope: this compares two attempts on this specific task, not the models’ overall capabilities. Sonnet is the project author’s identification of the earlier model; its exact version is not recorded here. The current session reports the model identifier openai/gpt-6-astra. The second pass had access to the paper, the earlier output, and explicit feedback that the figure was wrong; this was not an identical-prompt, controlled benchmark.

What worked Created the figure tab, integrated it with the site, and provided an accessible mathematical narrative and interactive Plotly charts. Retained the page’s purpose, but added a source-versus-result comparison, an executable solver, and explicit validation.
Methodology The page described SciPy SLSQP with a smooth gravity field and 15 intervals, while claiming an exact reproduction. This differed from the paper’s numerical method. Implemented Appendix A’s discrete dynamics, tangent-line gravity regions, binary region selection, and 32-sided thrust polygons using HiGHS MILP solves.
Reading the evidence Followed the written zero initial velocity and step count without identifying their conflict with the plots. Inspected the PDF graphics: Figure 4 starts near +200 m/s downrange, and Figure 3 contains 15 state points—14 intervals. Separated stated parameters from inferred ones.
Trajectory and thrust Downrange decreased immediately, the horizontal axis was reversed, and thrust stayed at 2 m/s² for 14 of 15 intervals before a small final increase. These did not reproduce the published profiles. Recovered the initial rightward motion to roughly 32 km downrange and the subsequent turn toward touchdown. Thrust rises toward the upper bound around mid-flight, consistent with the published braking pattern.
Validation The committed page contained numerical arrays and qualitative checks, but no accompanying Python solver, test suite, or measured Figure 3 comparison. Added 11 passing automated tests, boundary and dynamics residuals, solver-gap checks, and a node-by-node comparison: 47 m RMS and 103 m maximum difference.
Confidence and limitations Asserted that the result was globally optimal and that the theorem guaranteed the plotted discrete solution, without adequate numerical support. Distinguished fixed-time MILP optimality from continuous-time theory. Reported the inferred outer polygon’s small thrust-bound violation and the unresolved final-time discrepancy instead of claiming complete replication.
Presentation tradeoff Interactive charts offered hover inspection, but depended on an external Plotly CDN. Local SVGs work offline and show the original, reconstruction, overlays, and diagnostics. They trade away interactive hover in favor of portable figures and downloadable full-precision data.

The position differences are against vertices extracted from the published PDF, whose drawing precision is on the order of tens of metres—not against the authors’ raw arrays. Those trajectory vertices are used only for comparison after solving, never as an optimization target. There is no directly comparable published error metric for the Sonnet attempt, so these numbers are not a model-to-model error ratio.

Closer to Figure 3 does not mean every discrepancy is solved

The matching reconstruction holds the reported final time at 490 s, while an independent search on the reconstructed model finds a lower cost near 451.9 s. It also uses an inferred outer thrust polygon, permitting a norm of 10.0484 m/s² rather than exactly 10 m/s²; the text specifies an inner polygon. These are documented departures and unresolved differences, not evidence that the original continuous-time problem has been reproduced exactly.

What I learned from the comparison

On this task, Sonnet was useful for getting the website and explanation started. The current pass did better at auditing the source, exposing inconsistent inputs, and leaving behind a runnable experiment with checks. The practical lesson is to ask an AI coding assistant for parameter provenance, a reproducible solver, an original-versus-computed comparison, and explicit failure checks—not just a convincing plot and a confident explanation. This case does not establish which model is generally better or more efficient; comparable time, token, and cost measurements were not collected.
